\chapter{Introduction}\label{ch:introduction}

According to the World Economic Forum's Global Risks Report 2026,
cyber insecurity is listed among the top ten global risks and is projected to rise significantly from position 30 to position 5 in the long term,
highlighting the growing impact of \gls{AI} technologies~\cite{WEF2025GlobalRisks}.
This global perspective is further underlined by the CrowdStrike Global Threat Report which highlights a significant rise in \gls{AI}-enabled adversaries~\cite{CrowdStrike2025Report}.
These developments, combined with a shortage of cybersecurity experts and the rising complexity of systems and business models~\cite{Figueredo2024RCVaR},
emphasize the increasing need for automated tools and frameworks capable of identifying and mitigating emerging cyber threats.

To address some of these issues, \gls{NSAK}
\textthreequartersemdash~a modular, open-source network security scenario automation framework \textthreequartersemdash~
was established in a predecessor project~\ref{sec:predecessor-project-project-2}.
The framework aims to simplify the creation of automated security testing and
adversary emulation scenarios in controlled network environments, by providing reusable building blocks.

While our prior work demonstrated that it is feasible to automate red and blue team activities,
it also revealed the limitations of this approach.
Manually engineered scenarios offer modularity and reproducibility,
but lack adaptability to changing conditions and the development still requires deep cybersecurity expertise.
By contrast, \glspl{LLM} are able to reason over the current context and dynamically adjust their behavior depending on the environment they operate in.

The central hypothesis is that the integration of agentic \gls{AI} into the framework
can improve adaptability, flexibility and the degree of automation compared to static scenario implementations.
Simultaneously, it may reduce the operator's required depth of expertise to interpret the results and
plan follow-up steps in an attack or defense sequence.

This thesis approaches the following research questions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1:}\label{rq1} How does an agentic \gls{AI} scenario perform compared to a static reference scenario in network reconnaissance?
    \item \textbf{RQ2:}\label{rq2} To what extent can agentic \gls{AI} support operators during interactive network reconnaissance?
    \item \textbf{RQ3:}\label{rq3} To what extent is the implementation suitable for deployment in real-world environments?
\end{itemize}

% Approach
To address these questions, an \gls{AI} agent based on \gls{LangChain} is integrated into the framework and
provided with capabilities for autonomous system interaction and operator support.
With these features, single- and multi-agent reconnaissance scenario variants are developed,
which can be run interactively and autonomously, using models of various sizes.

The evaluation is structured around three perspectives corresponding to the research questions:

\textbf{Autonomous Scenario Comparison (RQ1)}~\textthreequartersemdash~A static reference scenario chaining host discovery, port scanning, and service enumeration serves as a baseline for comparison.
To reduce the number of variables in the evaluation, a reproducible test environment is specified and built with \gls{Containerlab}.

\textbf{Interactive Scenario Execution (RQ2)}~\textthreequartersemdash~Besides the direct comparison this work also wants to investigate how agentic \gls{AI} can interactively support an operator during network reconnaissance activities.

\textbf{Real-World Usability (RQ3)}~\textthreequartersemdash~Real-world usability is evaluated by testing against a physical setup of the evaluation environment and in the BFH network security lab.

The thesis is structured as follows:
Chapter~\ref{ch:related-research} reviews related work and analyzes how selected security frameworks integrate \gls{AI} into their products.
Chapter~\ref{ch:concepts} introduces the fundamental concepts and outlines the key design decisions that form the basis of this work.
Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} describes the research methodology, including the approach used for evaluation and data collection.
Chapter~\ref{ch:implementation} presents the implementation of the proposed solution, followed by
Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation}, which evaluates its effectiveness in virtual and physical test environments.
Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion} discusses the results and their implications, while
Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion} concludes the thesis and highlights potential directions for future work.


\section{Use of AI}\label{sec:use-of-ai}
Generative \glsxtrshort{AI} tools were used as aids in writing, image generation, animation, text to speech and engineering tasks throughout this thesis.
The conception, writing, and claims remain our own.
For the thesis, the following \glsxtrshort{LLM}s were used: ChatGPT, Claude, and Grammarly to code for language and grammar correction and rephrasing for clarity.
Approximately 80\% of the text passages were revised using these tools (February~\textthreequartersemdash~June 2026).
No references were cited that we had not read and checked for ourselves.
Complicated papers were summarized to help with faster information gathering.
Gemini was used for the animated background in the video and ElevenLabs was used for converting text to speech for the voiceover (June 2026).
